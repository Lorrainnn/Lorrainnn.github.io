A.2: Recover Homographies

Establishing point correspondences

I manually selected corresponding points between overlapping images using the correspondence tool. In most cases, I selected more than four pairs depending on the available features.

The numbers of pairs I selected were:

  • Building: 25 pairs for building1 → building2 and 25 pairs for building3 → building2.
  • Indoor: 11 pairs for indoor1 → indoor2 and 18 pairs for indoor3 → indoor2.
  • Shelf: 18 pairs for shelf1 → shelf2 and 13 pairs for shelf3 → shelf2.

Homography equations and least squares

A homography is a 3×3 matrix that maps points from one image to another:

p′ ∼ Hp

Since H has 8 degrees of freedom, at least 4 point correspondences are required. Each pair provides two equations. I set the bottom-right entry to 1:

H = [[a, b, c], [d, e, f], [g, h, 1]]

For corresponding points (x, y) and (u, v), the equations are:

u = (ax + by + c) / (gx + hy + 1),    v = (dx + ey + f) / (gx + hy + 1)

Rearranging gives a linear system A h = b. I used np.linalg.lstsq() to solve for the 8 unknown parameters and then reshaped them into the 3×3 matrix.

A.3: Warp the Images

Given a homography H, I used inverse warping. For every output pixel, I used the inverse homography to find its location in the original image:

p ∼ H−1p′

I implemented nearest-neighbor sampling and bilinear interpolation. Nearest neighbor samples the closest pixel, while bilinear interpolation uses a weighted average of four neighboring pixels.

Image rectification

I selected four corners of a rectangular object in each image and mapped them to a front-facing rectangle. The point order was top-left, top-right, bottom-left, bottom-right.

A.4: Blend the Images into a Mosaic

I used the middle image as the reference and warped the other images into its coordinate system. I first calculated the canvas size using the transformed image corners, then applied inverse warping with bilinear interpolation.

For blending, I used distance-based feathering. Pixels closer to the image edges get smaller weights, while pixels farther from the edges get larger weights. Finally, I combined the warped images using weighted averaging to reduce visible seams.

Small alignment differences and ghosting can still be visible where the photographs do not overlap perfectly.

A.5: Bells & Whistles — Cylindrical Projection

I implemented cylindrical projection to reduce distortion in wide panoramas. Each image is mapped onto a cylindrical surface using inverse warping and bilinear interpolation.

Effect of focal length

I tried f = 800, 1200, and 1800 on the building, indoor, and shelf images. For the shelf, a smaller focal length made the wooden edges look too curved, so I used f = 1800. I kept f = 1200 for the building and indoor images.
